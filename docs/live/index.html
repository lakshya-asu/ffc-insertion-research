A closer look at the opening.

We added a Basler ace 2 a2A2448-75ucBAS camera with a Kowa LM35JC10M 35 mm lens. The lens front sits 100 mm from the focus target, above the cable at a 25° elevation. Your Arducam reference remains useful for wider views; this camera is for the last few millimetres of approach.

What this gives us

2448 × 2048 pixels over a calculated 17.65 × 14.77 mm field, measured in a plane perpendicular to the optical axis. At 0.38× magnification, sampling is 7.21 µm per pixel. These figures describe image scale, not positioning accuracy.

The focus tradeoff

The starting lens setting is f/8. A thin-lens estimate gives about 0.42 mm total depth of field with a one-pixel blur criterion, before diffraction. We therefore keep the entrance near the focus plane and retain a wider camera for the earlier approach.

The first, diagonal placement made focus vary across the opening. The selected view looks straight across its width while staying above the cable. Compare both below, including the blur sensitivity render. These images have no learned masks or privileged control overlay.

What changed in the connector, and what is assumed?

The source CAD has one fused connector mesh and no independently movable latch. The manufacturer identifies a sliding actuator. We retained the PCB and replaced that connector with an engineered simulation substitute: separate housing rims, bottom contacts and a sliding part.

The 11.7 mm-wide, 0.5 mm-high slot and 0.8 mm actuator travel are explicit assumptions. They are not manufacturing tolerances. The slider's appearance changes with position, but it does not yet clamp the cable. Finger contact, cable deformation and insertion are not part of this static experiment.

Why acceptance still does not allow insertion

The two heads share a pretrained encoder and training data; they can agree on the same error. Their softmax and agreement scores are ranking signals, not calibrated success probabilities. Thresholds were selected on calibration data and evaluated once on the test set. Test mistakes remain failures; thresholds have not been quietly adjusted to remove them.

The same fixed native-pixel windows and simulated Arducam reference remain in use. Blur is a digital image stress transform, not a measured lens model. Jaw boxes, static cable shapes, ideal optics and community CAD still limit realism. The connector-mouth geometry, latch state, actual lens focus and cable deformation need physical validation.

Every runtime output keeps motion disabled and leaves insertion pose and latch state unknown. The next physical gate is measuring and imaging a real connector entrance and cable end at the intended working distance, followed by a gripper occlusion sweep. Reliable component review is useful evidence for that work, not permission to skip it.

THE DESIGN DISCUSSION

The choices shaping this system.

Open a decision to see its rationale, the tradeoff and the evidence that would make us change it. These are working decisions, not a finished hardware specification.

01Start with two fixed cameras

Decision. Use a desk overview to find the cable and a board close-up to resolve the two ribbon sockets. The published baseline used ideal 768 × 512 pinhole cameras. The live preview now uses the selected Arducam reference described above.

Why. One overview does not give enough pixels to judge a tiny socket or an exposed cable end. Fixed mounts also make the first calibration and evaluation easier to understand.

Tradeoff. The close-up usually cannot see the loose cable, and neither camera yet represents a purchased lens or measured noise model.

Revisit when. Real images establish the required pixel scale and show where gripper occlusion prevents a usable tip estimate. That may justify another close-up or a wrist camera.

02Learn image regions before commanding motion

Decision. Train a small U-Net to segment cable body, contacts, stiffener, CSI and DSI. Keep the robot stationary.

Why. We can inspect the model’s errors directly and establish that it uses RGB before combining perception with actions.

Tradeoff. A region or centroid does not supply the physical end frame, face orientation, socket mouth or uncertainty needed for insertion.

Revisit when. Endpoint-specific estimates pass held-out real-camera checks against the task’s measured clearance. The next skill is bounded pickup, followed by tactile retention checks.

03Keep simulator truth outside runtime perception

Decision. Simulator labels are offline supervision and evaluation data. The inference container mounts RGB images, a frozen checkpoint and three code files.

Why. Those are inputs we can replace with real-camera images in the lab. Hidden cable or connector poses would invalidate that transfer.

Tradeoff. Ambiguous views must stay ambiguous. Later control needs uncertainty, touch and recovery instead of a simulator-state shortcut.

Evidence. The inference launcher has no label or USD mount, no network and no GPU device. A repeated isolated replay checks prediction stability. This does not yet qualify live synchronization.

04Improve the board with traceable geometry

Decision. Keep the community Pi 4 STEP and official Camera Module 3 STEP. Add 102 small package footprints recovered from the official board drawing, seven approximate labels and materials for existing connector solids.

Why. Board context helps distinguish the CSI camera socket from the similar DSI display socket. Small parts and labels provide visual structure missing from the first import.

Tradeoff. Package height/type and surface finishes remain assumptions. The extra detail does not prove photorealism.

Revisit when. Photos and measurements of the actual board revision reveal geometry, marking or reflectance mismatches. Inspect the before-and-after scene.

05Separate cable appearance from cable mechanics

Decision. Preserve drawing dimensions and opposite exposed faces while varying a static, length-preserving ribbon curve for perception.

Why. We can start testing appearance without claiming an unmeasured deformation model.

Tradeoff. The curve is authored; it has not settled under gravity. This dataset cannot validate bending, friction, buckling or grasp retention.

Revisit when. We measure laminate response, stiffener transitions and contact behavior. A reduced or FEA-informed model must reproduce held-out shapes and forces before contact learning.

06Freeze the model before opening the test set

Decision. Develop on 96 training scenes and 24 validation scenes, with two images per scene. Render 56 new evaluation scenes after checkpoint selection.

Why. Test failures should describe the frozen model rather than guide silent tuning on the benchmark.

Tradeoff. The same assets and camera placements appear across splits. These results measure synthetic scene generalization only.

Revisit when. We add new board/cable specimens and real images. The test protocol then needs explicit specimen and environment splits, larger failure sets and uncertainty calibration.

07Keep the phone connector as its own second task

Decision. Stage 1 is Pi ribbon insertion into an upright CSI socket. Stage 2 is a phone-style flex with a small connector pressed down onto the PCB.

Why. They share observation and robot infrastructure, but need different gripping surfaces, mating mechanics and completion checks.

Tradeoff. The second connector pair is not selected. Hirose BM28 is only a representative candidate, not a claimed phone part.

Revisit when. A matched plug/receptacle, handling specification and replaceable research coupon are chosen. Read the Stage 2 plan.

Why these positions?

Give the robot an inspection pose

After pickup, bring the reinforced end into C2’s calibrated field. Inspect the contact face, tip edge and grip position, then rotate to see the other face. A fixed inspection camera keeps its geometry stable and avoids adding a bulky camera to the tool. The first render had weak cable/background contrast. The revised study adds a dark backdrop 100 mm behind the presentation plane and a diffuse area light. Their mounting and gripper clearance still need verification. This is a proposed free-space presentation skill, not a requirement for a passive regrasp fixture; we can still compare fixture-assisted handling later.

Watch insertion from both broad faces

The Pi CSI socket accepts the ribbon downward. C3 and C4 look across the upright ribbon from opposite sides, leaving the overhead approach volume open. Their views can reveal different edges when the ribbon or fingers hide the socket. They are complementary views, not an ordinary parallel stereo pair: opposite cable faces do not automatically provide matching features for triangulation.

Keep tactile contact behind the free end

The proposed pinch surface contacts the reinforced region while leaving the leading edge visible and free to enter the socket. A full-size tactile module may be too bulky here. We must measure the cable’s usable stiffener length and design a thin jaw or remote optical readout around it; the rendered blocks only test an assumed envelope. Measure slip and grip retention before transferring to contact control.

Put force sensing in the board’s load path

Support every Pi standoff through one plate carried by a six-axis transducer. A parallel support, taut harness or hard stop touching during normal operation can bypass the sensor. Strain-relieve the board wiring, characterize residual harness forces, and add overload stops outside the normal measurement travel. Mount the vacuum sensor near the suction tip to reduce plumbing delay; fuse pressure with image/tactile evidence rather than treating suction as proof of a stable grasp.

Use controlled light and calibrated timing

Provide diffuse overhead light for pickup and independently adjustable lights at the inspection and socket views. Test crossed polarization on glare-prone surfaces while preserving contrast on metal contacts. Start with global-shutter cameras, fixed exposure and synchronized timestamps; calibrate intrinsics, the multi-camera geometry and the robot frame. The study now includes an authored inspection backdrop and area light. Their intensity is not calibrated radiometry; polarization, physical exposure and timing still need bench validation.

What must pass before we freeze the mounts?

  1. Replace the jaw envelope with the selected actuator, fingers, tactile package and cable routing. Sweep the full approach and retreat with the FR3, including camera bodies and brackets.
  2. Across pose, grip-depth, yaw and cable-bow variation, measure whether the leading edge and relevant socket boundaries remain observable. Include deliberate occlusions and require the estimator to stop or request another view when information is missing.
  3. Use real calibration targets and a real ribbon sample to measure scale, focus, glare, distortion and repeatability throughout the working volume. Start with a provisional 0.10 mm edge-localization development target; derive the final requirement from measured connector clearance.
  4. Validate tactile retention and fixture force noise independently. Train and evaluate the stronger vision model only after the sensor design has passed these checks.

NEXT MODEL COMPARISON

We should use stronger pretrained vision.

The U-Net is a reproducible first baseline, not a commitment to a small model for the finished robot. My preferred next comparison is a pretrained dense-feature backbone with task-specific segmentation and endpoint heads, alongside a promptable segmentation model for proposals and annotation assistance. How this live page works

A read-only service on the research machine publishes curated status and timestamped renderer snapshots. The page polls every two seconds. A paused renderer retains its last frame; the status explicitly changes to a saved frame as it ages. Dataset capture can publish new scene views as it runs.

The public connection uses a temporary Cloudflare Quick Tunnel. It works while the machine and tunnel service are running; the hostname can change after a tunnel restart. The GitHub page remains the stable entry point. No terminal, arbitrary files or robot command endpoint is exposed.
